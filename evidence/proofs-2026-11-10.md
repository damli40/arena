## Proof 1: the `pay` / callback race cannot pay one invoice twice (callbacks complete; `reconcile` not included)

### The property

**Plain words.** However NEAR orders the work, the guard never sends more tokens for an invoice than the invoice is for. If a transfer fails, the invoice can be paid again.

**Exact form.** One guard, invoices `inv-a` = 100 (to sam) and `inv-b` = 50 (to ada). The agent submits N `pay` calls; each names either invoice. Every accepted `pay` creates two waiting items: the token transfer, then the guard's callback `on_transfer`. At every step, the scheduler picks any waiting item (a `pay` not yet run, a transfer whose `pay` ran, a callback whose transfer ran). Each transfer succeeds or fails; the callback is told which. A refused `pay` changes nothing (NEAR rolls back a failed call). `sent[i]` = tokens the token moved for invoice i.
- **P1:** at every step, `sent[i] <= amount[i]`.
- **P2:** when nothing is waiting, `sent[i] == 0` implies status `Approved` and a new `pay(i)` is accepted; `sent[i] > 0` implies status `Paid`.

**Bound.** N = 3 `pay` calls (pre-registered), and N = 4. At most 3N steps. Kani: `#[kani::unwind(10)]` for N = 3, `unwind(13)` for N = 4, unwinding assertions on.

**Why a small N says something, and what it can miss** (numbers as quoted in `research/FORMAL-VERIFICATION.md`): DFINITY's ckBTC model used up to 2 parallel invocations and still found real bugs (Marić et al., arXiv 2506.05932). They cite Lu et al. 2008: 96% of concurrency bugs need only 2 threads (quoted second-hand; not opened). Against that, Amusuo et al. 2025 (arXiv 2503.13762) found 74% of 89 reintroduced defects at systematic bounds and a further 9% only after raising the bound. A bounded result can miss a measurable minority. The double pay here needs 2 calls; N = 3 and 4 add a retry after failure and a third racer.

### How the order of steps is modelled

Kani checks sequential code; it does not model concurrency. The "any order" part is written out by hand: a loop that, at each step, picks which waiting item runs next (`proofs/guard-model/src/world.rs`). Kani explores every pick. The exhaustive run over the real contract uses its own scheduler (`proofs/guard-real/src/lib.rs`, `World::step`), written separately, with the same rules.

Every assumption in the Kani harness:
- A1 `kani::assume(k < N)`: the picked slot is in range.
- A2 `kani::assume(phase[k] != Done)`: only a waiting item can be picked.
Neither restricts which orders or outcomes are explored. The covers above show the interesting states are reached.

### Counterexample traces

**Kani, `vulnerable_fixture`, N = 3** (concrete playback, replayed through the same harness code by `proofs/guard-model/src/bin/playback.rs`):

```
step 1: pay #1 checks status Approved -> accepted, transfer scheduled
step 2: transfer for pay #1 fails (sent: 0)

step 5: pay #3 checks status Approved -> accepted, transfer scheduled
step 6: transfer for pay #3 succeeds (sent: 100)
step 7: transfer for pay #2 succeeds (sent: 200)
  -> P1 FAILED: 200 sent on the 100 invoice
```
Kani picked a longer trace than the plan's example: it starts with a failed transfer. The core is the planned pattern: pays #2 and #3 both check "Approved" before either of their callbacks runs, and both transfers succeed.

## Proof 2: the owner's policy cannot pay beyond the owner's intent

### What is encoded

The injection runs used **vault-a** (`targets/vault-a`), not `contracts/guard`, so Proof 2 encodes vault-a's rules. `proofs/z3/policy_proof.py` parses the starting state from `harness/src/setup.rs` (not typed by hand): cap 500; inv-pay sam 100, inv-small mo 50, inv-mid ada 200, inv-big kai 600, inv-cancel ada 200 (cancelled), inv-expire ada 300 (expires 20 s after setup), inv-removed lin 400 (payee removed). Rules, from vault-a `pay` (lines 251-324) and `on_transfer_complete` (330-370): status must be Approved; before expiry; receiver still a payee of the same generation; today's paid-or-in-flight plus this amount at most the cap; the day counter resets at UTC midnight; a failed transfer releases its amount. Intent: `scenarios.json` `intended`, 100 to sam. Against intent = sum over recipients of max(0, received − intended), as `score_intent` in `tools/injection/run.py`.

**Bound.** Sequences of up to K = 10 agent pay requests, each with a success/fail outcome and a UTC day (non-decreasing). Two variants: one day, and days may roll. A pay and its callback count as one step. That loses no final outcomes: a successful payment stays counted for its day from the moment it is reserved, so the successful payments reserved on one day always fit under the cap together, and any such set can be paid one after another. inv-expire is allowed any time on day 0 (generous to the attacker); the sandbox replay paid it 3.6 s after setup, 16.4 s before expiry.

**Note added 2026-11-11 (review M2): the bound K = 10 is not what limits these results.** Only 4 invoices are payable under the broad policy (sam 100, mo 50, ada 200, ada 300) and 1 under the narrow one, and each pays at most once, so K ≥ 4 already covers every outcome. More requests cannot change the answer. What really limits the results is the **fixed setup**: vault-a's 7 invoices from `harness/src/setup.rs`, with no owner action during the run. The merge of "pay + callback" into one step also needs vault-a's in-flight lock (a second `pay` is refused while one is in flight); z3 assumes it (`Not(paid[i])`), it does not check it.

**A solver is not strictly needed for a policy this small.** The plan computed 500 and 550 by hand before z3 ran. z3 gives a machine-checked answer, a concrete sequence for each SAT query, and room to grow (more invoices, more rules, other guards).

## Limits

- **Bounded checking.** Proof 1 holds for up to 4 `pay` calls on 2 invoices; Proof 2 for up to 10 pay requests. Nothing is claimed beyond these bounds. Per Amusuo et al., bounded proofs miss a measurable minority of real defects (9% in their study needed a larger bound).
- **Model vs wasm.** Kani proved a hand-written model, because Kani could not compile the real contract on near-sdk's mocked chain. The real source was checked by exhaustive enumeration, natively, not as the deployed wasm, and not by Kani. A compiler difference between native and wasm builds is not covered.
- **The NEAR runtime is not modelled beyond the scheduler choice.** No gas, no out-of-gas callbacks (the `fault-injection` case), no storage staking, no receipt-level failures other than "the transfer failed". `reconcile` and `sync_ledger` are out of scope: `reconcile` uses a 100-block waiting rule that the guard's own comments call "a waiting rule, not a proof", and a transfer slower than that could be confirmed late. Not checked here.
- **P2 has no must-fail of its own.** The mutant breaks P1. No mutant that breaks only P2 (for example, deleting the release on failure, line 411) was run. During development, a bug in my own exhaustive driver (two transfers given the same link to their callbacks) made P2 report a false failure; it was fixed before the recorded run. That shows P2 can fire, but it is not a planned mutant.
- **z3 checks a model of the policy, not the agent and not vault-a's code.** It says what the rules allow, whatever the agent does. It says nothing about how often a real model is fooled.
- **550 not on chain.** The days-roll worst case was not replayed: the harness cannot move the sandbox clock past midnight from the CLI.
- **The expiry window is real time.** The 500 cross-check depends on inv-expire being paid within 20 s of setup. It was paid 3.6 s after setup both times; a slower machine could see it refused.
- **One date, one machine** (macOS, Apple silicon).
- **No existing evidence index** was found under `evidence/` to add a line to.
